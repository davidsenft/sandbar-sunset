How this works

Two levels on the crossing, one band of light, and a rule that fits in a sentence.

1 · The crossing

The Wingaersheek bar is a spit joined to the beach by a low saddle. Mapping NOAA's 1/9-arc-second coastal elevation model of the flats, then correcting it against an evening spent out there in August 2026, puts that saddle at roughly 0.9 ft above MLLW — while the bar's own spine runs higher, so there's a real window where the bar is bare sand and the neck you cross is still shin-deep. Two levels matter: below about 1.4 ft the crossing is ankle-deep at most and you can simply walk it; below about 2.4 ft it's a knee-deep wade, and the bar has broken the surface. Deeper than that and there's nothing out there to wade to yet. Those are the two bands on the chart.

2 · The rule

A rating is a statement about where the tide curve sits relative to the two crossing levels and the golden band. Nothing is weighted or averaged; an evening either satisfies a rule or it doesn't.

Three suns — golden hour opens while the crossing is in the wadeable band (under wade, still over walk), and the half hour after sunset is walkable throughout. Nothing is required of the half hour before sunset; that's when the wading happens.
Two suns — the half hour either side of sunset is walkable throughout.
One sun — that same hour is wadeable throughout.
Anything else is left off the list.

3 · The light

Sunset is computed for the beach itself (42.650° N, 70.684° W) with NOAA's solar algorithm, checked against the US Naval Observatory. The golden band on the chart is the seventy minutes before the sun goes down.

Why it can see so far ahead

Tides are astronomy. Predictions come from NOAA's harmonic forecasts for station 8441571 — Annisquam, Lobster Cove, a mile up the river from the flats, and run through December 2040. Three suns means the evening has the full shape described above — about fifteen a year, six of them in swimmable weather. Use the date box above to pull up any evening, including the ones that don't make the list, and see for yourself.

A note from the sea: the flats flood from the edges first when the tide turns, and faster than you'd think. The saddle you crossed is the first thing to go. Wander far, but keep an eye on the channel behind you.

Honest about error bars: the saddle height carries roughly ±0.4 ft of datum uncertainty, the elevation survey dates from 2021, and sandbars migrate — so treat the depths as a good guide, not a guarantee. Predictions also ignore weather entirely: wind, storm surge, and clouds on the horizon do what they like. Times are local to Gloucester. Compiled July 2026.
